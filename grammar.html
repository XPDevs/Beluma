1. Sounds & writing

23 letters: a b d e f g h i j k l m n o p r s t u v w z x. Five vowels with one sound each; the remaining letters are always pronounced the same way.

LetterIPAHint
a e i o u/a/ /e/ /i/ /o/ /u/fa, bed, see, for, food
j/j/yes
r/r/rolled r
x/ks/box
w/w/wet
othersas writtenb d f g h k l m n p s t v z

The acute accent marks the stressed syllable: béla, nésa. The accent does not change the sound or the meaning — it only tells you where the stress falls. Faster Beluma text often leaves accents out.

Syllables are open-favouring: ne-sa, bi-no, fura-nela-sula. No bleeding consonant clusters; words flow.

Beluma is written with the 23 Latin letters shown above — one letter, one sound, in speech or in print. The acute accent (béla) is a stress mark only, written to help learners; ordinary text may leave it out and lose nothing but a hint.
Accents & homophones. Because an accent only marks stress, an accented word and its unaccented twin sound identical. Placement is not always a matter of the accents "being dropped" — Beluma happily lets the same spoken form carry more than one meaning. ra and rá are the same spoken word, meaning both near and (colloquial) was; da/dá cover if and did; mila is at once thousand and my; koro is the bear and the interjection really?. Listener and reader pick the sense from context — the translator shows every listed sense of a homonym in its analysis panel (e.g. thousand / my).

2. Sentence structure

Order is Subject – Verb – Object, exactly like English:

Siva leva mi. — She loves me.
Mi féma fura. — I eat fruit.

The word le = “the”, o = “a/an”. They are optional but normal.

Lé fira hura. — The bird flies.

Noun phrases

Adjectives come before the noun; possessors come before the noun too:

réva gino — a happy girl  ·  béla lomas — beautiful trees  ·  mi-la nésa — my house  ·  tu-la lavo — your dog

Gender is not grammatical in Beluma. sélo = wolf, sélo-niva = she-wolf — natural gender is marked only when it matters.

5. Verbs & tense

Verbs are never conjugated for person or number. Time is the only thing the verb cares about, and it is marked with a small word that comes before the verb.

MarkerMeaningExample
(none)presentmi gai — I go / I am going
népastmi né gai — I went
wéfuturemi wé gai — I will go
wésfuture with “about to”mi wés gai — I am about to go
unnegative (not)mi un kéma — I don't know
un + né/wénegative past/futuremi un né gai — I did not go
dunegative command (Don't!)du gai! — Don't go!
kvoyes/no questionkvo tu gai? — Are you going?
Mi né féma fura. — I ate fruit.  ·  Lé fira wé hura. — The bird will fly.

A verb used without a “name” noun is still a verb. Add -va to make a noun form of a verb (the “doing” form): sola-va = playing (as a noun), lé-tova-va = holding.

6. Adjectives & adverbs

Adjectives sit before the noun. Every adjective can act as the noun of that quality: béla = beautiful (and: beauty), sulo = strong (and: strength).

Adverbs are adjectives + -lé:

gavo = good → gavo-lé = well  ·  réva = happy → réva-lé = happily  ·  sulo-lé = strongly

Comparison uses mesa (more) and susa (most) before the adjective; “than” is ban:

Nivo s mesa sulo ban mi. — He is stronger than me.

Real compounds from the dictionary

nela-sula = water + great = ocean  ·  fura-sulo = fruit + great = root  ·  kiva-nira = work + small = gadget  ·  loma-sulo = tree + great = great forest / brown  ·  luma-saro = light + star = flashlight  ·  kéro-nira = mind + small = idea

Coining new words

Any two roots can be pressed together to make a new word. This is how Beluma grows: the dictionary is the starting set, not a closed list. A new compound is legal the moment its roots are legal.

nela-fura = water + fruit = juice  ·  fira-nira = small bird  ·  luma-pira = light-tool (lamp)  ·  kiva-siva-nira = work + voice + small = phone  ·  néla-fura-pira = water + fruit + -pira tool = watering can  ·  kiva-nira … make your own

The translator (translator.html) analyses any root+root compound automatically and shows the word's parts in the analysis panel — coin a word, type it, and see it understood. If you keep using one, add it to h.txt so everyone else can too.

Tens and hundreds stack head-first: vinta-kvín = 25, cénta-tuo-tro = 123. Ordinals add -ji: onaji = first.

10. Questions & negation

Yes/no: put kvo at the front.

Kvo tu gai po nésa? — Do you go home?  ·  Yes = kék, No = non.

Wh-words: want = kva (which/what), kvasi (who), kwaras (where), kvado (when), kvaso (why), havi (how). They start the sentence.

Kvado tu gai? — When do you go?  ·  Kvasi s ta? — Who is that?

Negation: un before the verb makes it negative; du makes a command negative.

Mi un kéma ta. — I don't know that.  ·  un + réva = un-réva (sad).

12. Formal vs Colloquial Beluma

Beluma has two registers, like many natural languages. Both are correct — you choose based on context.

Formal Beluma (writing, speeches, education):
Words kept separate: mi-la nésa na  ·  Regular tense markers: né gai, né s  ·  Full sentences, careful pronunciation.
Colloquial Beluma (daily speech, friends, family):
Fused words: mila nésana  ·  Irregular high-frequency verbs: vára, rá, dá  ·  Contractions, faster speech, discourse markers (evi, koro, maka).

What follows shows how the colloquial register behaves in detail.

Fused words (Grammaticalisation)

In rapid speech, small words stick together. You will often hear natives say:

nésana (at home) instead of nésa na  ·  mila (my) instead of mi la  ·  wégai (will go) instead of wé gai

These are perfectly correct in spoken Beluma, though formal writing keeps the words separate.

Irregular high-frequency verbs

The most commonly used verbs have developed irregular past tenses because speakers memorise them as single chunks rather than building them with the né marker:

gai (to go) → vára (went)  ·  s (to be) → rá (was/were)  ·  diva (to do/make) → dá (did/made)

Topic marking & flexible word order

While the formal rule is Subject-Verb-Object, speakers use the particle ya to move the most important information to the front of the sentence for emphasis:

Formal: Mi né féma fura. (I ate fruit.)  ·  Colloquial: Fura ya, mi né féma. (The fruit, I ate it.)

Discourse markers

Conversations include padding and hesitation, just like any natural language:

evi = well... / um (used when thinking)  ·  koro = really? / is that so? (used to show you are listening)  ·  maka = actually / in fact (used to politely correct someone)

Idioms & collocations

Much of colloquial Beluma is spoken in fixed chunks that no longer decompose literally (see the idioms at the end of h.txt):

méra-nako = thanks very much (lit. sea of thanks)  ·  luma-kúna = dizzy (lit. light head)  ·  nula-vosa = goosebumps (lit. cold flesh)  ·  tuma-sulo = courage (lit. heart-great)

13. Relative clauses & linking ideas

Relative clauses let one sentence carry another. Beluma opens them right after the noun, and the clause keeps perfectly normal SVO order — nothing moves to the front:

  • ka = who / which / that — subject relative (the noun is doing the action)
  • ta = that / which — object relative (the noun is the object of the clause); this is the same word as the demonstrative “that”, exactly like English
  • ka-la = whose — possessive relative (lit. of-who)
Lé misi ka s unréva. — The person who is sad.
Lé fura ta mi né féma. — The fruit that I ate.
Lé keran ka-la lavo né rusa s unréva. — The man whose dog ran away is sad.

Note: the question word is a separate, older root — kvasi = who? (Kvasi s ta? — Who is that?). The relative word ka means “who/which” only inside an added clause. Beluma thus tells the two jobs apart, even though English uses the same word for both.

Linking ideas

Sentences join up with plain function words — no rearrangement needed:

kausa = because (Mi s esa, kausa mi né rusa. — I am tired because I ran.)  ·  ekse = therefore (Mi s esa, ekse mi we gai nésa. — I am tired, therefore I will go home.)  ·  tamen = however (Lé néla s nula, tamen mi we néva. — The water is cold, however I will drink.)  ·  seke = then / next  ·  kondise = under the condition that
