Learn Beluma in 20 minutes

This quick course gets you speaking. Speak each example aloud — Beluma is meant to be spoken, and the writing is just the words made visible.

Lesson 1 · The alphabet

Beluma has 23 letters, and every letter makes exactly one sound. That makes it the easiest possible alphabet to master.

a b d e f g h i j k l m n o p r s t u v w z x

The vowels are the big five: a (a in father), e (like bless), i (like see), o (like for), u (like food).

Consonants are the easy part: read them exactly as written. j is the y in “yes”. r is rolled a little. x is “ks”.

An accent (béla) just tells you which syllable to stress. It's a guide, not a rule to memorize.

Read aloud: néla (water) · luma (light) · réva (happy)
Lesson 2 · First sentences

Beluma sentence order is exactly like English: who does what.

Mi = I, tu = you, siva = she, nivo = he.

s is the all-purpose “is/am/are/be”.

Mi s réva. — I am happy.
Tu s gavo. — You are good.
Siva s bela. — She is beautiful.

Action words (verbs) are the dictionary's “bare form”: féma eat, gai go, níva sing, léva love, héna see.

Mi léva tu. — I love you.
Nivo héna mi. — He sees me.
Lesson 3 · Time: past and future

Verbs never change their form. Time is a marker that sits before the verb. One marker = one job.

né = past · wé = future

Mi féma fura. — I eat fruit. (now)
Mi né féma fura. — I ate fruit.
Mi wé féma fura. — I will eat fruit.

And since verbs are easy, the two hardest English words translate in one step: wé gai = will go, né léva = loved.

You never have to memorize “ate, eaten, will eat”. In Beluma it's always fema with a marker in front.
Pro-tip: everyday speech. In formal writing, we use “né” before the verb for the past tense. But in everyday conversation, the three most common verbs have their own irregular past forms: vára (went), rá (was), and dá (did). Also, don't be surprised if you hear wégai instead of wé gai — in rapid speech, the future marker and the verb fuse together!
Lesson 4 · Making it plural and yours

Add -s to a word to make it plural. Add -la to make it “belonging to”.

lavo (dog) → lavos (dogs)
mi-la nésa — my home
tu-la lavo — your dog
siva-la fira — her bird
Drill: what is “my dogs”?
mi-la lavos
Lesson 5 · Buildings & physical things

Beluma builds specialty words from simple roots. This is the core of Beluma's vocabulary: learn a root, get a family of words free.

néla = water → néla-sula = ocean (water-great)
luma = light → luma-saro = flashlight (light-star)
kémor = teacher <- kéma (know) + -or (doer)
féma-pira = spoon (eat + tool)

Head-first: the important word comes first, the divider word follows.

Rule of thumb: imagine the English written as “Kingdom-land”, “water-bottle”, “fire-light” — that's exactly how Beluma does it.
Lesson 6 · Questions and orders

Turn any sentence into a yes/no question by putting kvo in front.

Tu gai. — You go.
Kvo tu gai? — Do you go? / Are you going?

Tell someone not to do something with du (a kind of “stop!”):

du gai! — Don't go!
du féma ta! — Don't eat that!

And un means “not” for normal sentences:

Mi un kéma. — I don't know.
Mi un wé gai. — I won't go.
Lesson 7 · Your first conversation

Put it together with a real exchange. Read both roles. Twice. Out loud.

Sala! Havi tu s?
Hello! How are you?
Mi s gavo. Nako. Unas tu?
I'm good. Thanks. And you?
Mi s réva. Kvo tu gai po nésa?
I'm happy. Are you going home?
Kék. Mi wé gai po nésa.
Yes. I will go home.
Ravi, krosa!
Goodbye, friend!

New words: krosa = friend, nako = thanks, unas = and, kék = yes, ravi = goodbye, havi = how.

Lesson 8 · Natural Speech & Shortcuts

Real Beluma speakers don't always talk like textbooks. In casual conversation, words fuse together and common verbs become irregular.

Colloquial fusions (fast speech):
nésana = at home (instead of “nésa na”)
mila = my (instead of “mi-la”)
wégai = will go (instead of “wé gai”)
Irregular past tense (high-frequency verbs):
gai (go) → vára (went)  [instead of “né gai”]
s (be) → rá (was/were)  [instead of “né s”]
diva (do) → dá (did)  [instead of “né diva”]

Example conversation (casual):

Sala! Havi tu s?
Mi s gavo. Mi vára po nésana.
Koro? Tu s réva?
Kék, mi s réva-lé!

Footnote: koro also means bear — the trailing question mark is what tells the reader it is the colloquial really?, not the animal. In speech the raised tone does the same job. For more on homonyms like koro, mila, rá and dá, see Grammar §1 (Accents & homophones).

These forms are perfectly correct in spoken Beluma. Formal writing keeps words separate and uses regular tense markers.
