## 1. Sounds & writing

    
23 letters: a b d e f g h i j k l m n o p r s t u v w z x. Five vowels with one sound each; the remaining letters are always pronounced the same way.
    
        
| Letter | IPA | Hint | 
        
| a e i o u | /a/ /e/ /i/ /o/ /u/ | fa, bed, see, for, food | 
        
| j | /j/ | yes | 
        
| r | /r/ | rolled r | 
        
| x | /ks/ | box | 
        
| w | /w/ | wet | 
        
| others | as written | b d f g h k l m n p s t v z | 
    
    
The acute accent marks the stressed syllable: b├®la, n├®sa. The accent does not change the sound or the meaning ÔÇö it only tells you where the stress falls. Faster Beluma text often leaves accents out.
    
Syllables are open-favouring: ne-sa, bi-no, fura-nela-sula. No bleeding consonant clusters; words flow.
    Beluma is written with the 23 Latin letters shown above ÔÇö one letter, one sound, in speech or in print. The acute accent (b├®la) is a stress mark only, written to help learners; ordinary text may leave it out and lose nothing but a hint.
    Accents & homophones. Because an accent only marks stress, an accented word and its unaccented twin sound identical. Placement is not always a matter of the accents "being dropped" ÔÇö Beluma happily lets the same spoken form carry more than one meaning. ra and r├í are the same spoken word, meaning both near and (colloquial) was; da/d├í cover if and did; mila is at once thousand and my; koro is the bear and the interjection really?. Listener and reader pick the sense from context ÔÇö the translator shows every listed sense of a homonym in its analysis panel (e.g. thousand / my).

    
## 2. Sentence structure

    
Order is Subject ÔÇô Verb ÔÇô Object, exactly like English:
    Siva leva mi. ÔÇö She loves me.
    Mi f├®ma fura. ÔÇö I eat fruit.
    
The word le = ÔÇ£theÔÇØ, o = ÔÇ£a/anÔÇØ. They are optional but normal.
    L├® fira hura. ÔÇö The bird flies.
    Noun phrases
    
Adjectives come before the noun; possessors come before the noun too:
    r├®va gino ÔÇö a happy girl ┬á┬À┬á b├®la lomas ÔÇö beautiful trees ┬á┬À┬á mi-la n├®sa ÔÇö my house ┬á┬À┬á tu-la lavo ÔÇö your dog

    
## 3. Nouns & plurals

    
        
| Form | Rule | Example | 
        
| singular | base word | lavo (dog) | 
        
| plural (-s) | add -s after a vowel | lavos (dogs) |

Gender is not grammatical in Beluma. s├®lo = wolf, s├®lo-niva = she-wolf ÔÇö natural gender is marked only when it matters.

## 5. Verbs & tense

    
Verbs are never conjugated for person or number. Time is the only thing the verb cares about, and it is marked with a small word that comes before the verb.
    
        
| Marker | Meaning | Example | 
        
| (none) | present | mi gai ÔÇö I go / I am going | 
        
| n├® | past | mi n├® gai ÔÇö I went | 
        
| w├® | future | mi w├® gai ÔÇö I will go | 
        
| w├®s | future with ÔÇ£about toÔÇØ | mi w├®s gai ÔÇö I am about to go | 
        
| un | negative (not) | mi un k├®ma ÔÇö I don't know | 
        
| un + n├®/w├® | negative past/future | mi un n├® gai ÔÇö I did not go | 
        
| du | negative command (Don't!) | du gai! ÔÇö Don't go! | 
        
| kvo | yes/no question | kvo tu gai? ÔÇö Are you going? | 
    
    Mi n├® f├®ma fura. ÔÇö I ate fruit. ┬á┬À┬á L├® fira w├® hura. ÔÇö The bird will fly.
    
A verb used without a ÔÇ£nameÔÇØ noun is still a verb. Add -va to make a noun form of a verb (the ÔÇ£doingÔÇØ form): sola-va = playing (as a noun), l├®-tova-va = holding.

    
## 6. Adjectives & adverbs

    
Adjectives sit before the noun. Every adjective can act as the noun of that quality: b├®la = beautiful (and: beauty), sulo = strong (and: strength).
    
Adverbs are adjectives + -l├®:
    gavo = good ÔåÆ gavo-l├® = well ┬á┬À┬á r├®va = happy ÔåÆ r├®va-l├® = happily ┬á┬À┬á sulo-l├® = strongly
    
Comparison uses mesa (more) and susa (most) before the adjective; ÔÇ£thanÔÇØ is ban:
    Nivo s mesa sulo ban mi. ÔÇö He is stronger than me.

    Coining new words

## 10. Questions & negation

    
Yes/no: put kvo at the front.
    Kvo tu gai po n├®sa? ÔÇö Do you go home? ┬á┬À┬á Yes = k├®k, No = non.
    
Wh-words: want = kva (which/what), kvasi (who), kwaras (where), kvado (when), kvaso (why), havi (how). They start the sentence.
    Kvado tu gai? ÔÇö When do you go? ┬á┬À┬á Kvasi s ta? ÔÇö Who is that?
    
Negation: un before the verb makes it negative; du makes a command negative.
    Mi un k├®ma ta. ÔÇö I don't know that. ┬á┬À┬á un + r├®va = un-r├®va (sad).

## 12. Formal vs Colloquial Beluma

    
Beluma has two registers, like many natural languages. Both are correct ÔÇö you choose based on context.
    
        Formal Beluma (writing, speeches, education):
        Words kept separate: mi-la n├®sa na ┬á┬À┬á
        Regular tense markers: n├® gai, n├® s ┬á┬À┬á
        Full sentences, careful pronunciation.
    
    
        Colloquial Beluma (daily speech, friends, family):
        Fused words: mila n├®sana ┬á┬À┬á
        Irregular high-frequency verbs: v├íra, r├í, d├í ┬á┬À┬á
        Contractions, faster speech, discourse markers (evi, koro, maka).
    
    
What follows shows how the colloquial register behaves in detail.

    Fused words (Grammaticalisation)
    
In rapid speech, small words stick together. You will often hear natives say:
    
        n├®sana (at home) instead of n├®sa na ┬á┬À┬á
        mila (my) instead of mi la ┬á┬À┬á
        w├®gai (will go) instead of w├® gai
    
    
These are perfectly correct in spoken Beluma, though formal writing keeps the words separate.

    Irregular high-frequency verbs
    
The most commonly used verbs have developed irregular past tenses because speakers memorise them as single chunks rather than building them with the n├® marker:
    
        gai (to go) ÔåÆ v├íra (went) ┬á┬À┬á
        s (to be) ÔåÆ r├í (was/were) ┬á┬À┬á
        diva (to do/make) ÔåÆ d├í (did/made)
    

    Topic marking & flexible word order
    
While the formal rule is Subject-Verb-Object, speakers use the particle ya to move the most important information to the front of the sentence for emphasis:
    
        Formal: Mi n├® f├®ma fura. (I ate fruit.) ┬á┬À┬á
        Colloquial: Fura ya, mi n├® f├®ma. (The fruit, I ate it.)
    

    Discourse markers
    
Conversations include padding and hesitation, just like any natural language:
    
        evi = well... / um (used when thinking) ┬á┬À┬á
        koro = really? / is that so? (used to show you are listening) ┬á┬À┬á
        maka = actually / in fact (used to politely correct someone)
    

    Idioms & collocations
    
Much of colloquial Beluma is spoken in fixed chunks that no longer decompose literally (see the idioms at the end of h.txt):
    
        m├®ra-nako = thanks very much (lit. sea of thanks) ┬á┬À┬á
        luma-k├║na = dizzy (lit. light head) ┬á┬À┬á
        nula-vosa = goosebumps (lit. cold flesh) ┬á┬À┬á
        tuma-sulo = courage (lit. heart-great)
    

    
## 13. Relative clauses & linking ideas

    
Relative clauses let one sentence carry another. Beluma opens them right after the noun, and the clause keeps perfectly normal SVO order ÔÇö nothing moves to the front:
    
        
- ka = who / which / that ÔÇö subject relative (the noun is doing the action)
        
- ta = that / which ÔÇö object relative (the noun is the object of the clause); this is the same word as the demonstrative ÔÇ£thatÔÇØ, exactly like English
        
- ka-la = whose ÔÇö possessive relative (lit. of-who)
    
    
        L├® misi ka s unr├®va. ÔÇö The person who is sad.
    
    
        L├® fura ta mi n├® f├®ma. ÔÇö The fruit that I ate.
    
    
        L├® keran ka-la lavo n├® rusa s unr├®va. ÔÇö The man whose dog ran away is sad.
    
    
Note: the question word is a separate, older root ÔÇö kvasi = who? (Kvasi s ta? ÔÇö Who is that?). The relative word ka means ÔÇ£who/whichÔÇØ only inside an added clause. Beluma thus tells the two jobs apart, even though English uses the same word for both.

    Linking ideas
    
Sentences join up with plain function words ÔÇö no rearrangement needed:
    
        kausa = because (Mi s esa, kausa mi n├® rusa. ÔÇö I am tired because I ran.) ┬á┬À┬á
        ekse = therefore (Mi s esa, ekse mi we gai n├®sa. ÔÇö I am tired, therefore I will go home.) ┬á┬À┬á
        tamen = however (L├® n├®la s nula, tamen mi we n├®va. ÔÇö The water is cold, however I will drink.) ┬á┬À┬á
        seke = then / next ┬á┬À┬á
        kondise = under the condition that
